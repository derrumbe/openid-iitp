IITP and WISE: two draft profiles, after the layering

Both drafts extend the Shared Signals Framework into non-human territory, and both arrived within three weeks of each other. On 2026-09-23 the two sets of proposers agreed a layering: WISE owns the confederacy of running workloads — federation, non-human credentials, trust anchors — and IITP owns the environment those workloads run in. Three IITP candidates were retired into WISE. One event, workload-compromised, is now deliberately shared under the same name from two different Transmitters.

The overlap, by event count

The three remaining overlaps

Two are near-homographs — the same event under a slightly different name, differing mostly by tense or scope. The third, workload-compromised, is the one place the layering deliberately leaves both profiles speaking: same event name, two Transmitters, two different vantage points.

The difference that isn’t about events

Counting events understates the split. The two drafts disagree about what a Shared Signals transmitter is — and that disagreement decides what a receiver is allowed to do on arrival.

Authority is why the layering works

WISE events are assertions by the party with standing to make them: a trust domain authority speaking about credentials it issued. IITP events are detections by a party with no standing at all — the draft says plainly that a receiver “MUST NOT treat these as commands.” The 2026-09-23 layering routes each event to the Transmitter best positioned to assert it. workload-compromised is the deliberate exception: both vantage points are useful, so both profiles carry it, and the Transmitter tells the receiver which is speaking.

Only IITP carries confidence

Because IITP transmitters are fallible detectors, every event may carry severity, confidence, and recommended_actions, and receivers are told to weight automated enforcement by them. WISE has severity on exactly two events and no notion of confidence — consistent with a profile where the transmitter is authoritative and simply correct. On the shared workload-compromised event this shows up directly in the payload: IITP’s version carries confidence and recommended actions, WISE’s does not.

They disagree on what a workload is called

WISE identifies subjects with wimse:// or spiffe:// URIs rooted in a trust domain. IITP invents an environment subject format and reaches for complex when it needs a workload — then puts spiffe_id inside the event payload rather than the subject. For the shared workload-compromised event, the two profiles carry the same event name but disagree about the subject, which is the one field a receiver routes on.

Neither can retract a compromise assertion

IITP has a consistent follow-up pattern — reverted, resolved, restored — but only for its three specified events. WISE only reverses on vulnerability status, via VEX. Neither can currently retract a compromise assertion, which matters most for the one event they still share.

What the layering resolved

The two drafts are no longer competing for the same territory. Of 24 distinct facts between them (10 IITP events, 20 WISE events, 3 pairs overlapping and one of those a deliberate shared-name pair), 21 are uncontested — nearly seven eighths. IITP owns the cloud control plane, tenant-level campaigns, and supply-chain integrity in the environment. WISE owns the credential and workload lifecycle, federation, trust anchors, and posture policy inside the trust domain.

Three earlier IITP candidates — federation-trust-compromise, secret-exposure, and trust-anchor-change — were retired in favour of the equivalent WISE events under the 2026-09-23 layering. IITP’s workload-compromise was renamed workload-compromised to align with WISE, and the two profiles now carry that event under the same name from two different Transmitters: IITP as an environment-level detection, typically supply-chain-driven; WISE as an authoritative statement by the trust domain issuer.

The authority split that produced the layering is the reason it holds: each event now sits with the Transmitter that has standing to assert it. On the shared event, the receiver reads the two versions as complementary rather than duplicative — the outside view and the inside view of the same fact.
